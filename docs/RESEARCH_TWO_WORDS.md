# The two-words stage: what children do, and what follows for this brain (survey, 2026-11-17)

A survey by a research agent during the day loop, kept nearly as returned. Tags: **[V]** checked in that session
(mostly abstracts or citing reviews), **[M]** from memory, **[I]** the surveyor's inference.

**Bottom line.** Exact counting is the wrong thing to be stuck on: most children under 30 months cannot do it either.
The other faults (one-word or garbled replies, answers that get longer and worse with free talk, repeating a heard
word) look like one fault: every heard unit is treated as both a target and a cue. An intake filter, a two-slot
frame producer and a separate rote store address it.

## 1. Small numbers

- Two core systems: a ratio-limited approximate magnitude system, and parallel tracking of up to about three objects
  (Feigenson, Dehaene & Spelke 2004) [V].
- Monkey LIP: neurons varying monotonically with numerosity, proposed as accumulator input to tuned number neurons
  (Roitman, Brannon & Platt 2007) [V]. A network trained only on object classification had 9.6% number-tuned units
  (Nasr, Viswanathan & Nieder 2019) [V].
- Number words are learned one at a time, in order. A one-knower gives one for "one" and a handful for every other
  word [V]. In privileged English-speaking samples the one-knower level is reached at 24–47 months and the cardinal
  principle at 34–51 months (Sarnecka 2014) [V]; a Belgian replication found one-knower at a mean of 39 months [V].
- **So [I]: at 18–30 months most children are non-knowers or one-knowers. 0.42 against a base of 0.35 is in range.
  The two-words stage should not be gated on counting.** An exact count needs every fixation's match to be right,
  so naming at 0.5 a thing compounds; "is there" needs only one.
- Mechanisms proposed: (a) the number of fixations opened as a code of its own, independent of naming; (b) the
  graded match per fixation summed, read by a few units tuned on the log of the sum; (c) group the fixated things
  by likeness first and pool the heard word's match over a group.

## 2. One word to two

- Brown's eight relations cover about 70% of early combinations (agent + action, action + object, entity + locative,
  possessor + possession, attribute + entity, recurrence "more X", nonexistence "X gone") [V].
- Braine 1976: a few independently acquired positional formulae generate most combinations, each a relational term
  plus a slot ("all gone ___") [V]. Lieven, Pine & Baldwin 1997: about 60% of multiword utterances fit lexically
  specific frames; the rest include frozen phrases [V]. At 2;1 only 26% of a child's novel multiword utterances
  needed more than one edit from an earlier utterance [V].
- Chunk-based learners from child-directed speech reproduce about 70% of child utterances across 29 languages
  (McCauley & Christiansen) [V]. Successive single-word utterances come before true combinations (Scollon, Bloom) [M].
- Mechanisms proposed: (a) a pivot cell: a unit heard often with many different neighbours in a fixed position
  becomes a frame with a slot, filled by the word cell most driven by what is seen or wanted; (b) a two-slot
  readout: after the first word its cell is refractory, the echo of the child's own word drives the second, and a
  learned stop ends the reply at about its usual length plus one; (c) two successive single-word turns that both
  appear, in order, in the adult's expansion are bound as a chunk.

## 3. What a toddler takes in from a long utterance

- Stressed and final syllables are kept far more than unstressed non-final ones (Echols & Newport 1992) [V].
  Mothers put the focused word at the end of the utterance on a pitch peak (Fernald & Mazzie 1991) [V].
  Two-year-olds drop weak syllables mostly from the beginning (Gerken) [V]. Imitation is selective: children imitate
  what they are just learning (Bloom, Hood & Lightbown 1974) [V].
- Mechanisms proposed: (a) only the last N units of an adult's reply are learned from, N the child's own mean
  length of utterance plus one; earlier units are ignored, not counted against; (b) learning weighted by a unit's
  salience (rarity, length, final position, whether what is seen drives its cell); (c) a whole tail is learned only
  if it holds at most one unknown unit.

## 4. Routines

- Routines such as "bye-bye" are among the first things acquired and carry little reference [V]. Spontaneous use is
  rare in preschoolers; parents prompt and children comply (Greif & Gleason 1980) [V]. A "gestalt" route stores
  unanalysed chunks whole and segments them later (Peters 1983) [V]. Repeating the last heard word in answer to a
  question is normal toddler echolalia [M].
- **So [I]: whole prompt → whole reply, by rote, in a store apart from the slow statistics of cues.**
- Mechanisms proposed: (a) an episodic routine store keyed by the echo of the whole question with nothing seen, the
  adult's reply as one chunk, strengthened by repeats; (b) a heard unit may excite other cells but not its own, with
  a short suppression of the question's units when answering; (c) "say thank you" reuses the echo skill.

## What I take from it (2026-11-17)

1. **Counting leaves the two-words stage** (it goes to preschool with the photo questions). This is the training
   program corrected by how children are, not a lowered bar: "how many dogs?" is a three-year-old's question.
2. **An intake limit for leisure talk**: of a reply that was not the answer to a lesson, only the last few units are
   learned from (as many as the child's own usual reply plus one). Measured the same day: the length of the parent's
   replies, not the talk, is what costs the answers (docs/LEISURE.md).
3. **Routines by rote**: the episodic store built today already keys moments with the eyes closed by the echo of
   what was heard; that is mechanism 4(a). To be measured on the social routines.
4. **Pivot frames** are the mechanism for real two-word speech; not built. The two-words text lessons are scored
   about 0.33; whether a pivot-and-slot readout is what they need is the next question on the text side.
