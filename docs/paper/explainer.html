What we built, the one idea that carries it, and what did nothing

A map of the project for reading before the paper. Every number comes from the project's own logs as of 23 November 2026.

The story in four steps

  1. We built a small learner out of brain-like parts. It has an ear, a visual cortex, cells that learn words, a memory for single episodes and a mouth. It never uses backpropagation.

  2. Shown the CIFAR-10 pictures once each, it names 69% of pictures it has never seen. A network of the same shape trained by backpropagation names 62%.

  3. We then removed the parts one at a time. The win did not come from our learning rule or from the filters we raised by hand. It came from one preprocessing idea, decorrelation, applied twice.

  4. Given the same decorrelated code, an ordinary gradient-trained reader does just as well as ours. So the claim the paper can make is about the code, and it cannot be about the rule.

What decorrelation means

It does not add information. It reshapes the code so that a simple learner seeing each example once can use what is already there.

  • Why the code is lopsided. Neighbouring pixels look alike, and most filters respond to the same big things: brightness, contrast, large shapes. So a few directions in the code are very strong and the rest are faint.
  • Why that hurts a one-pass learner. A simple rule that sees each example once learns fast along the strong directions and barely along the faint ones. The faint ones are where a cat differs from a dog. More passes helped little (+3 points for three passes) and bigger steps made it diverge.
  • Why "double". We decorrelate in two places. Step 1 works on the raw 5 × 5 patches before the filters. Step 2, the relay, works on the 4,096-number code after the filters. The relay alone is worth about 10 points (0.591 → 0.687).
  • How we know it adds nothing new. A statistical method that decorrelates internally (streaming LDA) scores about the same with or without our relay (0.722 and 0.741, one seed).
  • The weak spot. Both steps are computed in one batch calculation before learning starts. A brain would have to learn them with local rules. Our first local version, a published lateral-inhibition network, recovers about four fifths of the relay's gain (0.513 → 0.605 against 0.627, three seeds, first 10,000 pictures).

Hebbian or random filters: where they sit

Only in vision. Text never passes through filters.

What a filter is

A filter is a small 5 × 5 pattern detector. The visual cortex has 256 of them and slides each one over the picture. A filter answers strongly where the picture matches its pattern.

Speech takes the other path: the ear groups bytes of text into units. There is nothing Hebbian-versus-random about that path.

The two ways to get filters

Random. The 256 patterns are drawn by chance and never change.

Hebbian. They start random. Then the cortex looks at unlabelled pictures, and for each patch the filter that answers most moves a little towards it. No labels and no error signal are involved.

What we found

For our learner it made no difference: 0.687 with Hebbian filters, 0.693 with random ones.

The Hebbian filters are not empty. Streaming LDA gains two points from them (0.739 against 0.719). Our simpler reader cannot use whatever that is.

Why, as far as we know

Hebbian filters come out more selective, but many are near copies of each other: 658 pairs, against none among random filters. Copies add no new directions to the code.

That random patches are hard to beat has been known since 2011. We found it again the slow way.

What the paper can claim

It can say

A learner with no backpropagation can learn from a single pass, and here is how well.

The single-pass advantage of this kind of learner comes from a decorrelated code. We show it with the controls that would have proved us wrong: a gradient reader, random filters, and the relay removed.

A fast store beside slow weights gives one-shot memory of an instance, and it has to be filed by what the eye was on.

Most of what we built did nothing, and we report which parts and how we know.

It cannot say

That local learning rules beat backpropagation. Ours tie with a gradient reader on the same code.

That the learner is fully local. Two steps are batch calculations.

That Hebbian filters matter. For this learner they do not.

That it is a model of a child, or that any of this holds at scale.

Running now

  • The fair fight. Backprop given the same relay step, tuned the same way, on three seeds. It decides how much of the 7-point lead survives.
  • Lives with no pass mark. Three learners that leave a stage only when their curve flattens. At 40,000 moments they are still in first words and still rising (0.43, 0.46, 0.49).
  • Kinder or stricter adults. Queued next: lives where a good try ("woof" for a dog) earns reward all life long, and lives where it counts early and less with age, against the strict lives. The tests stay strict in every arm.
  • The local relay on the whole stream. So far it has been run on the first fifth.
