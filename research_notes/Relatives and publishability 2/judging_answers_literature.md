## 1. How parents respond to wrong or imperfect answers

### 1.1 Explicit approval follows meaning, not form

- **Brown & Hanlon (1970)**: parental approval and disapproval did not depend on whether the child's sentence was well formed; they depended on whether it was true. The textbook examples ("He a girl" -> "That's right"; "Walt Disney comes on Tuesday" -> "No, he does not") are **[R]**, not found in a source this session. The finding itself is confirmed by two later papers that restate it:
  - Demetras, Post & Snow (1986): "mothers neither provided responses of approval and disapproval differentially to well-formed and 'primitive' (ill-formed) child utterances" **[V]**.
  - Hirsh-Pasek, Treiman & Schneiderman (1984) abstract: replicated it; parents "seemed attuned to the semantic value" of the speech **[V, abstract]**.
- **Demetras, Post & Snow (1986)**, J. Child Lang. 13, 275-292. Four mother-child pairs, children about 2 years old **[V]**.
  - Of adjacent maternal responses, on average 15% were explicit approval, 3% explicit correction, 26% clarification questions and 50% "move-ons" (conversation simply continues) **[V]**.
  - Per child, explicit approval was 14 / 10 / 19 / 14% and explicit correction 5 / 5 / 0 / 3% **[V]**.
  - "almost all explicit maternal responses were directed to the meaning or truth value of the child's utterance"; the one exception was approval for saying "please" **[V]**.
  - Explicit responses were not tied to well-formedness: corrections were equally or more likely after well-formed utterances, and approvals somewhat more likely after ill-formed ones **[V]**.
  - Implicit responses were tied to form: move-ons were the main response to well-formed utterances in all four dyads, clarification questions the main response to ill-formed ones in three of four. Exact repetitions followed more well-formed than ill-formed utterances in all four dyads **[V]**.
  - Six judges could not reliably say whether an implicit response targeted a semantic, syntactic or pragmatic problem; only phonological errors could be attributed **[V]**. So this literature cannot give a clean "meaning error" versus "form error" rate for implicit feedback.
  - Source: https://talkbank.org/childes/access/Eng-NA/0docs/demetras1986.pdf
- **Hirsh-Pasek et al. (1984)**: 40 dyads, 10 per age group from 2 to 5; mothers repeated ungrammatical sentences more than grammatical ones for 2-year-olds only **[V, abstract]**. The usual figures (about 21% versus 12%) are **[R]**.
  - https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/abs/brown-hanlon-revisited-mothers-sensitivity-to-ungrammatical-forms/49BB2206714FB41E1A1F1BECAD8936C9

### 1.2 How often form errors get a corrective reply

| Study | Data | Rate of corrective reply to form errors | Tag |
|---|---|---|---|
| Bohannon & Stanowicz (1988) | adults with 2-year-olds | 34% of all kinds of grammatical error | [V] as quoted in Saxton (2000) |
| same | same | about 90% of exact repetitions followed grammatical utterances; about 70% of recasts and expansions followed ungrammatical ones | [S] |
| Farrar (1992) | 7 morphosyntactic categories | 22% | [V] as quoted in Saxton (2000) |
| Morgan, Bonamo & Travis (1995) | Adam, Eve, Sarah | article errors recast about 49%; wh-question errors about 11% (26% for Eve) | [V] as quoted in Saxton (2000) |
| Saxton (2000) | Eve, 4,406 grammatical errors | 45.5% got negative evidence (correct form modelled right after the error), 23.9% negative feedback (clarification request), 56.1% one or both | [V] |
| Chouinard & Clark (2003) | 5 children 2;0-4;0 (3 English, 2 French) | on average 60% of early errors (before 2;6) reformulated in the next turn; rate falls with age | [V] as stated in Clark (2020) |
| Waller, Nozari & Yurovsky (2022) | 2,394 irregular-plural utterances, 223 children, CHILDES | 27.3% of errors reformulated; correct forms never reformulated | [V] |
| Hirsh-Pasek et al. (1984) to Chouinard & Clark (2003) | range across studies | 10% to 60% of errors | [V] as stated in Waller et al. |

- **Chouinard & Clark (2003)** details, from Clark (2020), Discourse Processes:
  - Reformulation rates did not differ statistically across phonological, morphological, lexical (word choice) and syntactic errors **[V]**. Wrong word choice is therefore reformulated about as often as wrong pronunciation.
  - Errors coded: Abe 2,911 of 6,276 utterances; Sarah 2,194 of 5,029; Naomi 1,095 of 2,242; Philippe 1,363 of 2,421; Grégoire 229 of 511 **[V]**.
  - 57% to 73% of reformulations were "side sequences" (the adult checks: "you mean X?"); the rest were embedded corrections **[V]**.
  - Children responded explicitly (repeat, acknowledge or reject) to 45% of reformulations **[V]**.
  - Adults reformulated erroneous utterances more often than they replayed error-free ones **[V, abstract]**.
  - https://web.stanford.edu/class/cs379c/class_messages_listing/curriculum/Annotated_Readings/ClarkDISCOURSE-PROCESSES-20_Unannotated.pdf ; abstract https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/adult-reformulations-of-child-errors-as-negative-evidence/AC1DA451B23BDDCE589273617D8B4F4C
- **Waller, Nozari & Yurovsky (2022)**, CogSci:
  - Parents replayed (repeated) 34.1% of correct plural forms and 16.1% of incorrect ones **[V]**. So a repeat is weak evidence of correctness; a reformulation is strong evidence of error.
  - Children were twice as likely to repeat their own error as to take up the correction **[V]**.
  - Children's own erroneous productions led to more errors later: they learn from what they themselves say **[V, abstract]**.
  - https://escholarship.org/uc/item/9c07b8px
- **Saxton (2000)**, First Language 20, 221-252. Child switched to the correct form on 7.8% of occasions after negative evidence, 6.6% after negative feedback, 6.5% after plain positive input and 2.9% after a move-on. Counting only from the point of 50% accuracy: 20.5% and 17.4% versus 9.5% and 7.1% **[V]**.
  - https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10002786/1/SAXTON2000Negative221.pdf
- **Nikolaus, Prévot & Fourtassi (2023)**, CogSci. 109,536 child-caregiver-child sequences, 664 transcripts, 127 children aged 12-48 months; 5.1% of children's initial utterances were ungrammatical **[V]**.
  - Clarification requests were less likely after grammatical utterances (beta = -0.516, SE 0.055) and acknowledgements more likely (beta = 0.2, SE 0.033) **[V]**.
  - Grammaticality improved in follow-ups after a clarification request (interaction beta = 0.603) **[V]**.
  - https://escholarship.org/uc/item/0zr7t0r1
- Golinkoff (1986): about 50% of infant-initiated interactions at 1;0-1;5 involved a repair started by the other person **[V] as stated in Clark (2020)**.

### 1.3 Do adults accept a wrong label?

- No verified rate found for parental correction of wrong object labels. This is the main gap of section 1.
- Chapman, Leonard & Mervis (1986), J. Child Lang. 13, 101-117: an experiment comparing three adult replies to overextended words (acceptance, correction with joint labelling, correction with explanation); the three differed in how well they changed the child's usage **[S, abstract]**. Acceptance is treated as a real parental behaviour.
- Mervis & Mervis (1988): demonstrating the defining attributes moved toddlers to the adult category most; purely verbal input played a minor role at first **[S, abstract]**. A citing source says mothers "seldom corrected" incorrect usage **[S, truncated snippet]**.
- A tertiary summary says parents "often respond to overextensions with acceptance" and that joint labelling reinforces the overextension **[S]** (https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_in_early_word_use).
- Against that: explicit approval and disapproval track truth (1.1), and lexical errors are reformulated as often as other errors (1.2).
- Reading of the evidence: adults do sometimes let a near-miss label pass (a "child-basic" category such as "kitty" for a lion), but when they respond explicitly they respond to truth. A wholly wrong label ("bear" for a dog) has no literature support as an accepted answer.

### 1.4 Do adults recast utterances they accept?

- Yes. The embedded correction is exactly this: confirm the meaning and model the form in one turn. Child: "il a des neus le camion". Mother: "oui, il a des pneus, pneus". Child: "pneus" **[V] example in Clark (2020)**.
- Demetras et al.: approvals were somewhat more likely after ill-formed utterances **[V]**.
- Correct utterances are also often replayed: 34.1% **[V]** (Waller et al.). Repeating the right answer after a right answer is normal adult behaviour.

## 2. Yes/no questions and the yes bias

- **Fritzley & Lee (2003)**, Child Dev. 74, 1297-1313. Four experiments, ages 2-5, comprehensible and incomprehensible yes/no questions about familiar and unfamiliar objects **[V, abstract]**.
  - 2-year-olds: consistent yes bias.
  - 3-year-olds: mixed, a transition age.
  - 4- and 5-year-olds: no bias on comprehensible questions, a no bias on incomprehensible ones.
  - Per-age bias scores from this paper were not obtained.
  - https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/14552399/
- **Fritzley, Lindsay & Lee (2013)**, Child Dev., questions about actions; 200 children aged 2-5 **[V]**.
  - Experiment 1 (30 per age), bias score from -3 to +3: 2-year-olds M = 1.51; 3-year-olds -0.83; 4-year-olds -0.96; 5-year-olds -1.25 (SE about 0.23) **[V]**.
  - Comprehensible questions M = 0.41, incomprehensible M = -1.18 **[V]**.
  - Experiment 2 (20 per age): 1.36, -0.23, -1.29, -1.39 **[V]**.
  - "I don't know" was 1.6% of all responses in Experiment 1; no 2-year-old used it, one third of 5-year-olds did at least once **[V]**.
  - Where no bias was found, children "were responding accurately, not randomly" **[V]**.
  - Scoring: yes score = number of "yes" to questions whose answer is yes; no score = number of "no" to questions whose answer is no; bias = yes score minus no score; one-sample t-test against zero; equal numbers of yes-correct and no-correct questions **[V]**.
  - https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3566352
- **Okanda & Itakura (2008)**, Int. J. Behav. Dev. 32. 108 Vietnamese and Japanese children aged 2-5. Bias = proportion yes-correct minus proportion no-correct, range -1 to +1, 0 = no bias; "don't know" and no answer get no score **[V]**.

  | Age | Familiar objects | Unfamiliar objects |
  |---|---|---|
  | 2 | .34 (SD .41), significant | .24 (SD .44), significant |
  | 3 | .27 (SD .44), significant | .18 (SD .47), marginal |
  | 4 | .22 (SD .25), significant | .10 (SD .34), n.s. |
  | 5 | -.00 (SD .15), n.s. | -.13 (SD .23), significant no bias |

  All **[V]**. Japanese 2-year-olds gave "no answer" on 43.1% of familiar-condition trials **[V]**. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2954668
- **Moriguchi, Okanda & Itakura (2008)**, First Language. 40 children, mean 49.3 months. Mean yes bias 0.16 (SD 0.24), above zero, t(39) = 4.19. It correlated with verbal ability (r = -0.47) and inhibitory control (r = -0.43), not with theory of mind (r = -0.13) or age (r = -0.20) **[V]**. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2954678
- Okanda & Itakura, naturalistic: strong yes bias in Japanese children aged 2 to 3;11 answering their own mothers at home; 3-year-olds can pick the right option when given two choices **[S]**.
- **Recency (last-word) bias, Sumner et al. (2019)**, PLOS ONE 14(6). For "A or B?" questions, toddlers answering aloud chose the second option 85.2% of the time; answering by pointing, 51.6% (chance) **[V]**. In CHILDES the bias lasts until about age 3, and children answered with one of the two options on 58.17% of such questions **[V]**. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6561545
- How widespread the problem is: 74% and 69% of developmental studies with 2- to 6-year-olds used questioning; yes/no was the most common question type (43.3% and 55%) **[V]**, in Fritzley et al. (2013).
- **How developmental studies control for it**:
  1. Balance yes-correct and no-correct items.
  2. Report a signed bias score and test it against zero.
  3. Leave out or separately count "don't know" and no answer.
  4. Add nonsense-word questions as bias probes.
  5. Use two-option or pointing formats, and counterbalance option order.

### Age norms

- ASHA 2 to 3 years: "Answers questions like 'What do you do when you are sleepy?' or 'Which one can you wear?'"; "Says their name when asked" **[V]**. https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-2-to-3-years/
- ASHA 19 to 24 months: uses and understands at least 50 words; parent tip "Ask your child to name pictures. They may not answer at first" **[V]**. https://www.asha.org/public/developmental-milestones/communication-milestones-19-to-24-months/
- ASHA 13 to 18 months: looks around when asked "where" questions; milestone ages are those reached by 75% of children **[S]**.
- Tyack & Ingram (1977): 22 children aged 2;0-3;11; yes/no, what and where questions already frequent at 2;0; rough order what, where, why, how, when **[S]** (production, not answering).
- Moradlou, Zheng, Tian & Ginzburg (2021), J. Child Lang. 48: wh-questions are understood earlier than yes/no questions (corpus plus German and Chinese elicitation) **[V, abstract]**. https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-child-language/article/whquestions-are-understood-before-polarquestions-evidence-from-english-german-and-chinese/D2AF188610CC3BF62D3E4D853EE3CEFF
- Grosse & Tomasello (2012), J. Child Lang. 39: 2-year-olds tell test questions (asker knows the answer) from genuine ones by situation **[V, abstract]**.
- Steffensen (1978): two children answered yes/no questions before understanding the question form or the words yes and no **[S]**.
- Not found: a percent-correct norm for "what is this?" or "is there a ...?" at 24 months.

## 3. Chance-corrected and baseline-relative scoring

- **VQA v1, Antol et al. (2015)**, arXiv 1505.00468 **[all V]**:
  - Accuracy = min(number of humans who gave that answer / 3, 1).
  - 38.37% of real-image questions are yes/no; 58.83% of those are answered "yes". "yes" is 22.82% of all answers, "no" 15.35%.
  - Test-dev, open-ended:

    | Baseline | All | Yes/No | Number | Other |
    |---|---|---|---|---|
    | prior ("yes") | 29.66 | 70.81 | 0.39 | 1.15 |
    | per question-type prior | 37.54 | 71.03 | 35.77 | 9.38 |
    | nearest neighbour | 42.70 | 71.89 | 24.36 | 21.94 |
    | bag-of-words, question only | 48.09 | 75.66 | 36.70 | 27.14 |
    | LSTM, question only | 48.76 | 78.20 | 35.68 | 26.59 |
    | deeper LSTM question + image | 57.75 | 80.50 | 36.77 | 43.08 |

  - Humans without the image: 40.81 overall, 67.60 on yes/no. With the image: 83.30 and 95.77.
  - The full model beats the question-only model by 9 points overall and only about 2 points on yes/no.
- **VQA v2, Goyal et al. (2017)**, arXiv 1612.00837 **[all V]**:
  - "tennis" is right for 41% of "What sport is" questions; "2" for 39% of "How many"; "yes" scores 87% on "Do you see a ..."; "Is there a clock" is "yes" 98% of the time.
  - Fix: complementary pairs, the same question on two similar images with different answers. About 1.1M pairs; answer entropy up 56%.
  - Test-standard: prior 25.98 (yes/no 61.20); language-only 44.26 (67.01); question + image 54.22 (73.46).
- **VQA-CP, Agrawal et al. (2018)**, arXiv 1712.00377. Train and test have different answer priors per question type **[all V]**:

  | Model | VQA v1 | VQA-CP v1 | VQA v2 | VQA-CP v2 |
  |---|---|---|---|---|
  | per question-type prior | 35.13 | 8.39 | 32.06 | 8.76 |
  | question-only LSTM | 48.23 | 20.16 | 43.01 | 15.95 |
  | SAN | 55.86 | 26.88 | 52.02 | 24.96 |
  | MCB | 60.97 | 34.39 | 59.71 | 36.33 |

  Yes/no accuracy of the question-only model falls from 79.05 to 35.72 on v1.
- NLI analogue: hypothesis-only models reach about 67% against a 34% majority baseline on SNLI **[S]** (Poliak et al. 2018; Gururangan et al. 2018).
- **Formulas**:
  - Cohen's kappa = (observed agreement - chance agreement) / (1 - chance agreement) **[V]** general form, in https://ar5iv.labs.arxiv.org/html/2401.03831
  - Informedness (Youden's J, binary case) = true positive rate + true negative rate - 1 **[R]** for the formula; that paper confirms the concept and that an always-majority guesser scores about zero **[V]**.
  - On GLUE a most-common-class guesser gets accuracy 45.7 but informedness 0.6 **[V]**.
  - Informedness is unstable on test sets under about 50 examples **[V]**.
  - Baseline-normalised score = (score - baseline) / (maximum - baseline) is the same form as kappa; no single canonical citation was found **[R]**.
  - Scott's pi is preferred over kappa and over percent agreement for judge-human agreement in Thakur et al. **[V]**.
  - The developmental bias score of section 2 is the field's own correction.
- **Reporting practice**: VQA papers print the blind rows in the same table, per question type, and readers compare by subtraction. No VQA paper found defines a single "margin above blind" number.

## 4. Language-model judges: practice and failure modes (to 2026)

- **Baseline reference**: Zheng et al. (2023), arXiv 2306.05685: position, verbosity and self-enhancement bias, limited reasoning; GPT-4 reaches over 80% agreement with humans **[V, abstract]**.
- **Short answers, Thakur et al., "Judging the Judges"**, arXiv 2406.12624v5. TriviaQA short answers, 13 judges, 9 answering models **[all V]**:
  - Humans agree with each other: Scott's pi 96.2, percent agreement 98.52%.
  - Best judges (GPT-4 Turbo, Llama-3 70B, Llama-3.1 70B): pi in the high 80s.
  - Small judges, pi on down-sampled data: Llama-3.1 8B 0.78; Mistral 7B 0.67; JudgeLM 7B 0.66; "contains" string match 0.64; Llama-3 8B 0.60; Llama-2 7B 0.47; exact match 0.47; Gemma 2B 0.26.
  - Percent agreement hides this: Llama-3 8B has pi 59 and still over 80% agreement; judges above 90% agreement can be off by more than 10 points in the score they assign.
  - "LLMs tend to judge positively when in doubt, and this is more pronounced for small models"; low-alignment judges "lack precision rather than recall".
  - Chance of saying "correct" when the answer is wrong (P+): Mistral 7B 0.87; Llama-2 13B 0.87; Llama-2 70B 0.99; Llama-3 70B 0.90; GPT-4 0.69; JudgeLM 7B 0.19.
  - By error type (GPT-4 / Llama-3 70B recall): wrong entity 98.3% / 96.6%; under-specified answer 33.9% / 23.3%.
  - Dummy answerers: judges cope with the question repeated back, but are "less robust when the responses are 'Yes' and 'Sure'".
  - Only the top judges gain from longer instructions; the others get worse with more instructions. Small judges are sensitive to reference order.
  - For ranking systems, simple string "contains" (rank correlation 0.99) and Mistral 7B (0.98) are as good as the big judges.
- **Leniency, Jain et al., "Beyond Consensus"**, arXiv 2510.11822 **[all V]**:
  - 14 judges, 366 student programs. True positive rate above 96% for most judges; true negative rate typically below 25% (best 53.5%).
  - Majority vote does not fix it (TNR 19.2%, maximum error 4.8%).
  - Minority veto (4 of 14 saying "invalid" is enough): maximum error 2.8%.
  - Regression correction fitted on a small human-labelled set: 1.2%.
- **Told the reference answer, still fooled: "One Token to Fool LLM-as-a-Judge"**, arXiv 2507.08794 **[all V]**:
  - Judges are given the reference, yet content-free answers are accepted. "Thought process:" gets false positive rates of 67.0% (Qwen2.5-72B), 73.0% (Llama-3 70B), 28.9% (GPT-4o). A bare ":" gets up to 24.4% on GPT-4o and 77.2% on Llama-3 70B.
  - Chain-of-thought and majority voting do not reliably help.
  - Fix: fine-tune the judge on 20k synthetic negatives (truncated openers labelled NO); false positive rate near 0%.
- **Reference quality, Krumdick et al., "No Free Labels"**, arXiv 2503.05061 **[all V]**:
  - Judge-human kappa: no reference 0.46; the judge's own reference 0.61; human reference 0.69.
  - On questions the judge itself gets wrong, pairwise: 0.30, 0.16 and 0.83.
  - A small judge (Qwen 2.5 7B) with a human reference can beat GPT-4o without one.
- **VQA short answers, Mañas, Krojer & Agrawal (2024), LAVE**, arXiv 2310.02567 **[all V]**:
  - Exact-match VQA accuracy is too strict: 16.33% of answers humans called correct scored under 0.5; the reverse 1.72%.
  - Why: multiple valid answers 34.25%, over- or under-specified 27.75%, synonym 21.0%, hypernym 5.0%.
  - Correlation with humans (Spearman): VQA accuracy 60.13; judge with Flan-T5 64.99; with GPT-3.5 68.91.
  - On yes/no questions the judge is worse than exact match: 88.40 (GPT-3.5) and 78.32 (Flan-T5) against 91.37.
  - Known error: over-accepting ("sink" for "what kind of sink").
  - Prompt recipe: rating 1-3; rationale before rating (66.74 versus 65.57); 8 examples (1 example: 59.07); separate example sets for yes/no and other questions; drop rare references.
- **Bias catalogue**: Ye et al., CALM, arXiv 2410.02736: 12 bias types; best judges keep their verdict under perturbation only 0.86 of the time **[S]**.
- **Reporting, Lee et al., "How to Correctly Report LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluations"**, arXiv 2511.21140 **[all V]**:
  - Corrected accuracy = (p + q0 - 1) / (q0 + q1 - 1), with p the raw judged-correct share, q1 the judge's sensitivity and q0 its specificity, both measured on a labelled calibration set.
  - A judge that accepts wrong answers inflates low scores: with q0 = 0.7 and q1 = 0.9, raw scores overestimate whenever true accuracy is under 0.75.
  - About 200 calibration items give a confidence interval under 0.1 in that example; fewer are needed for a better judge. Put more calibration items on the label the judge gets wrong more.
- **Judge as training reward**:
  - Policies trained against non-reasoning judges show judge score rising while true quality does not **[S]** (arXiv 2603.12246).
  - Judge biases pass straight into the reward and get exploited **[S]** (arXiv 2606.04923).
  - Pseudo-labelling fits its own wrong labels, "confirmation bias" **[S]** (Arazo et al. 2020, arXiv 1908.02983).
  - The child-language parallel is in 1.2: children's own errors lead to more errors.
- **Short-answer grading**: Chamieh, Zesch & Giebermann (2024, BEA): zero- and few-shot GPT and LLaMA are "not feasible for practical purposes" **[V, abstract]** (data include children aged 7-13 **[S]**). A 2026 meta-analysis of 890 results: decoder models trail encoders by about 0.37 in agreement **[S]** (arXiv 2603.04820).
- **Yes bias in models**: Dentella et al. (2023, PNAS) reported a yes-response bias in GPT-3-era models on grammaticality questions; Hu et al. (2024) showed it largely disappears with the humans' prompt **[S]**. Model- and prompt-dependent, not settled.
- **Not found**: any study of a model judge grading toddler-like one- or two-word answers, or of small judges on child data.

## 5. Lenient scoring in child-language work; echoing

- **Parent-report vocabulary (MacArthur-Bates CDI)**: credit a word even if mispronounced ("nana" for banana); credit a same-meaning alternative ("pop" for soda); do not credit a word the child only says in imitation **[S]** (supplemental instructions at sherlockcenter.ric.edu; the official manual was not read).
- **Whole-word phonological measures (Ingram 2002)**, from Taelman, Durieux & Gillis's note **[V]**:
  - pMLU: one point per segment produced, plus one for each correct consonant.
  - Proportion of Whole-word Proximity = child's pMLU / pMLU of the adult target. A graded "how close".
  - Proportion of Whole-word Correctness = correct attempts / all productions.
  - https://www.clips.uantwerpen.be/~gillis/assets/pdfs/pmlu.pdf
  - Percent Consonants Correct (Shriberg & Kwiatkowski 1982) **[R]**.
- **Expressive vocabulary tests**: the EVT is not a test of articulation; articulation errors are not penalised; a synonym or acceptable label counts **[S]**.
- **Language samples**: a 2001 JSLHR study tested removing imitative utterances, elliptical answers and one-word yes/no answers before computing MLU **[S]**. Bloom, Hood & Lightbown (1974): the share of imitative utterances differed widely across six children but was stable within a child **[S]**; figures not retrieved.
- **Computational models**:
  - Chunk-Based Learner (McCauley & Christiansen): scored by whether the model rebuilds the child's exact utterance **[S]**.
  - DevBench (Tan et al. 2024, arXiv 2406.10215): seven tasks; the score is closeness of the model's response distribution to children's (KL divergence), not accuracy; only one task (word association) is production **[V]**.
  - BabyLM 2025 pipeline: moved a generation task to probability comparison, so production is not scored **[S]**.
  - No model benchmark was found that scores open production leniently and guards against echoing.
- **Echoing**:
  - The human norm is to separate imitation from spontaneous speech and credit only the latter (CDI; language-sample practice).
  - Thakur et al. tested a "repeat the question" dummy answerer against judges **[V]**.
  - The toddler recency bias (85.2% second option **[V]**) makes "last word of the question" a developmentally real blind strategy.

## 6. Does the proposed design match the literature?

### Supported

1. **Lenient on form, strict on meaning** is the central finding of 1.1: explicit approval follows truth; form is handled by recasting.
2. **Closed versus open questions** matches the test-question versus genuine-question distinction, and the judge data: judges are reliable on a wrong entity (97-98%) and weak on under-specified answers (23-34%) and on yes/no (worse than exact match).
3. **Never confirm a wrong meaning** matches Demetras et al. and the self-training risk (Waller et al.; pseudo-label confirmation bias; reward-hacking papers).
4. **Margin above blind answerers** is standard VQA practice and equals the developmental bias-score logic. All three proposed blind answerers have a literature basis: always-yes (yes bias; VQA prior), most-common-answer (per-type prior), last word (recency bias).
5. **A fixed audit set** matches calibration practice.

### What should change

1. **Do not let the model judge decide polarity, number or object identity.** For closed questions the answer set is tiny. Decide meaning by code (yes/no polarity, the number, an allowed-name list with synonyms) and use the judge only for the form question "is this string an attempt at that word?". Evidence: yes/no is where judges are worse than exact match; reference-given judges still accept content-free answers at 5-77%; "Yes" and "Sure" are the dummy answers judges handle worst.
2. **Use three or four verdicts, not two.**
   - Meaning right, form fine: accept.
   - Meaning right, form off: accept and recast (say the conventional form back).
   - Meaning wrong: do not confirm; give the right answer.
   - Cannot tell: treat as not confirmed and ask or model. Parents use clarification questions in about a quarter of replies; judges otherwise "judge positively when in doubt".
3. **Always model the right answer in teaching, also after an accepted answer.** Parents replay 34% of correct forms; the embedded correction confirms and models at once. This removes the path by which an unnoticed wrong acceptance teaches the mistake.
4. **Label the audit set by a person, not only by a stronger judge.** Stronger judges share the leniency (TNR under 25% across 14 judges; majority vote does not help). Human references and a small human calibration set are what the literature shows to work. A few hundred one-word answers is a small job.
5. **Build the audit set mostly from wrong answers.** Leniency is a true-negative problem. Include yes-for-no, no-for-yes, wrong label of the same category, hypernym ("animal" for dog), echo of the question, last word, empty, filler ("sure", ":"), and the learner's own typical errors. Report the judge's sensitivity and specificity separately, plus Scott's pi or kappa. Do not report percent agreement alone.
6. **Correct the reported score with the judge's measured error rates** (Lee et al. formula) and give a confidence interval. Pass a stage on the lower bound of the margin, not the point estimate.
7. **Strengthen the blind baselines.**
   - Add a learned question-only baseline: the same learner with the picture blanked. In VQA it is 15-20 points stronger than the fixed priors (44-48 versus 26-30).
   - Take the best blind baseline per question type, not one global number.
   - For yes/no, report true-yes rate + true-no rate - 1; it is zero for always-yes and always-no.
   - Keep yes and no balanced within each question wording, and use complementary pairs (same question, two pictures, opposite answers) with credit only when both are right.
   - Add a changed-priors test split (VQA-CP): hand-made blind baselines miss learned priors.
   - Counterbalance option order in "A or B" questions.
   - Under about 50 items per type these margins are noise.
8. **Decide the near-miss policy explicitly.** The literature is thin on wrong labels. Hypernyms and child-basic labels are the real grey zone (27.75% of exact-match failures are over- or under-specification; judges catch only 23-34% of under-specified answers). Proposed: hypernym or close sibling means not confirmed, with the right word modelled; a wholly different category is wrong.
9. **Keep the small judge's prompt short and example-led.** Give the reference, 4-8 examples including rejected ones, separate examples for yes/no. Long rubrics lower agreement for small judges. If several judges are used, use a minority veto on acceptance rather than a majority vote.
10. **Count abstention separately.** Toddlers almost never say "I don't know" (1.6%), but no answer is common (43.1% in one group). Score silence as its own outcome, not as wrong meaning.
11. **Record the yes bias and recency bias as observations in their own right.** Human reference points: +0.24 to +0.34 on the -1 to +1 scale at age 2, about zero at 5; second option 85.2%. The margin handles credit; the bias score is a separate descriptive number.

### Open gaps

- No verified rate for parental correction of wrong object labels.
- Chouinard & Clark (2003), Bohannon & Stanowicz (1988) and Fritzley & Lee (2003) per-condition tables not read directly.
- No published work on model judges for toddler-like answers; the audit set would be new evidence.
- No answering norm (percent correct) for "what is this?" and "is there a ...?" at 24 months.
